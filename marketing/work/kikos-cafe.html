01 · Positioning

Not the rarest cards. The cutest ones.

The Pokémon card market is crowded and mostly chases rare, expensive pulls. We pivoted away from that to focus purely on cute “jank” cards: cheap, charming and aimed mainly at girls who collect for the art, not the resale value. Prices are kept competitive so a drop feels like a treat, not an investment.

My thinking

Rare-card shops compete on price and on who has the hottest pull, and we can’t win that. But lots of collectors, especially girls, buy cards because the art is cute, and those cards are cheap and overlooked. Picking that niche meant less competition and a clear reason to choose us.

The bet: a smaller, specific audience that loves what we sell will show up more reliably than a big, general one.

02 · Channels

Each channel has one job.

Instead of posting the same thing everywhere, each channel does a different part of the work, and every one of them points to the next.

My thinking

Instagram and TikTok are good at getting found but bad at running a fast sale. Telegram is the opposite: a small group that gets notified the moment a drop goes live. So instead of choosing one, I gave each a job and made every post point to the next step.

03 · Brand & content

A café, not a card shop.

The whole shop speaks in café language. Cards are “poke cuties” on a menu, every release is a drop with a theme, and the tone is soft and playful (“hello loveliesss”). It makes a simple product feel like a place people want to visit.

Every listing follows the same format: one card per post, shot straight on, then an emoji, the card name, rarity and set number, the price and quantity, and one call to action: comment “mine” to add it to your tray. When a card goes, the post is edited to qty: SOLD, so late visitors can see at a glance what’s left.

Every drop has a theme, and the shoot follows it

For our flower drop, we took the cards to Gardens by the Bay and matched each one to the real flowers in its artwork: Skitty among pink hydrangeas, Ribombee in a bed of lilac ones. The card and its setting tell the same story, which makes even a $1 card feel special and gives every post a reason to stop scrolling.

My thinking

A $1 common card has nothing to say on its own. Matching it to real flowers turns the listing into a small story, and the theme gives the whole drop something to tease. It costs a trip and a shoot, but the data later showed themed drops earning about four times more.

04 · Drop playbook

Tease, reveal, drop.

Drops are announced with a date and built up with teasers, so the audience knows when to show up. The current “sleepy cutie” drop opened with a Snorlax sneak peek a few days ahead and a promise of more peeks to come.

05 · Results

In the green, and we know why.

Since April we’ve run 19 drops, sold 1,086 cards and brought in $1,778.40. After every dollar spent on stock, sealed product and postage, and after paying out consignors, the shop is $842 in profit, a 47% margin on revenue. Weekly revenue has held between roughly $45 and $70 from August to September.

The clearest lesson is about themes. Our best drops were all themed: pink cuties pt 2 brought in $103.90 and pink cuties $92.70, followed by the rodent and fairy drops at around $79–86 each. The early untitled drops (“drop 1”, “drop 2”, “drop 5”) earned $23–25 each. Same audience, same prices; a story and a colour palette made a drop worth about four times more.

What changed because of it: every drop now follows a theme, with a name, a teaser and a photo shoot to match.

06 · Running it on data

Every decision is backed by data.

Claims, sales and stock used to live in comments and notes. My partner built an admin site for Kiko’s Studio with inventory, sales, expenses and claims in one place. It tags every sale to its drop, tracks weekly revenue, and reconciles what we actually spent against what we sold, including consignor payouts, so “profit” means real money, not just sale price minus cost.

It also shows where sourcing pays off. Every buying trip (tradeshows, markets, bulk lots) gets its own tag, so we can compare what we spent there with what those cards earned. Some trips have already paid back; others are still negative while their cards sit in stock, which tells us where to buy next time. As the one picking cards, this is how I learn which themes and sourcing trips are worth repeating.

Trade-offs

The calls I made, and what they cost.

Decision

How do people buy?

  1. ✓

    Comment “mine” to claim, in Telegram (chosen)

    Fast and exciting, like a live sale, with no checkout to build. Costs: claims have to be tracked by hand.

  2. ✕

    A web store with checkout (not chosen)

    Tidier, but slower to set up and it loses the drop-night buzz.

Decision

How do we release stock?

  1. ✓

    Themed drops on a set date (chosen)

    A theme gives us something to tease and a reason to show up. Costs: we have to hold stock back until it fits a theme.

  2. ✕

    List cards as they come in (not chosen)

    Less planning, but nothing to announce, so each post gets less attention.

Decision

How should listings look?

  1. ✓

    One fixed format every time (chosen)

    Same photo style, same fields, same call to action, so buyers can scan a drop in seconds. Costs: less room to experiment per post.

  2. ✕

    Write each post freely (not chosen)

    More personality per post, but harder to scan and easier to make mistakes.

07 · Fixing the bottleneck

Growth was blocked by manual work.

Claim-by-comment is great for excitement, but every claim had to be logged by hand and every listing uploaded by hand before each drop. Buyers had to scroll through comments to see what was still available. More customers would have meant more chaos, not more sales.

We weighed two fixes. A browsing webpage would look nicer but wouldn’t remove the manual logging, so the priority became a bot that logs claims automatically. Next on the list is a loyalty card with stamp-based discount tiers, so repeat buyers are rewarded without us tracking it by hand. I also built a small Vue app to plan binder layouts using the Pokémon TCG API.

08 · What I’d test next

Questions I want data on.

Which themes win

Colour themes (pink cuties) vs Pokémon types (rodents, fairies): which sells more per card listed?

Timing

Do evening drops sell out faster than lunchtime ones?

Loyalty

Once stamps launch, does the repeat-buyer rate go up?

Each test has one change and one number to watch, so we learn something even when the answer is “no difference”.
